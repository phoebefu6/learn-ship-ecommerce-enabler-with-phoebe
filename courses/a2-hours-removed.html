Margin comes from hours removed

Session 1 ended with a bucket: fees flow in, hours drain out, and the drain is the one pipe Serai controls. Tonight that becomes a sequencing rule you can defend in a budget meeting. Where tonight sits in the leader track

Session 1 drew the money map: three fee lines, three payers, a cost base made of 220 people's weeks. Tonight is the consequence. If the cost is hours, the margin is the hours you stop spending, and a data roadmap for an enabler should be ordered by how many hours each use case removes, not by how impressive it sounds. That is the rule. The rest of the session is about being honest with it: sizing it from numbers your finance lead would recognise, seeing what happens when the bench puts it up against three other orders, and learning the one input that turns the rule off. Part 1 · covers the sequencing rule and why a fee-based business obeys it

The sequencing rule, applied to a fee-based business

6 min live

Two routes lead from a data use case to Serai's margin. One goes through the brand's sales: the use case lifts GMV, and Serai keeps its share of the lift. The other goes through Serai's own rota: the use case takes over repeatable work, and the hours it removes are cost that never leaves the building. Put one hundred dollars down each route and watch how much arrives.

LiveWhy a service-fee business finds its margin in the drain3 min▶

Go back to Serai's two big fee lines from session 1. Retainers: 48 brands at $6,000 a month, $864,000 a quarter. GMV share: 2 percent of $45 million, $900,000 a quarter. Nearly half of Serai's money does not move when a brand sells more, and the half that does moves by $2 on every extra $100. Meanwhile the whole cost base is people: the content studio, the service desk, the store operators, the ad buyers. Every hour on the rota costs Serai about $18 loaded (constructed), and every hour that a machine takes over is $18 that stays in the bucket.

Now put a data use case on each route. A pricing model that lifts a brand's GMV by two percent is a fine thing for the brand. On Serai's fee split, two percent of $100 is $2 of GMV, and Serai's share of that is four cents. A content engine that drafts listing copy in five languages does nothing for anyone's GMV directly, but if it removes 420 of the studio's 1,200 hours a week, that is $7,560 a week that stops leaving. The routes are not close, and the reason is structural: Serai is paid mostly for effort, so the lever that pays Serai is effort.

This is why an enabler's data roadmap should not start where most data roadmaps start. Forecasting, pricing and the single customer view are the first three slides in almost every vendor deck, because they were built for businesses that own the goods or the buyer. An enabler owns neither. Its first slide should be the rota.

LiveThe playbook's rule, and its status2 min▶

The general form of tonight's argument is one line in the ship playbook: which use cases pay depends on how the business makes its money. Session 5 of that course, The revenue model decides, walks it across several revenue models; tonight applies it to exactly one, the fee-plus-share model of an enabler. Read that session for the frame, then come back here for the arithmetic.

Be straight about what kind of statement the rule is. It is a working heuristic: a rule practitioners find useful and can explain, not a finding from a study, and it is uncited in the playbook for that reason. Nothing tonight upgrades it. What the bench in Part 2 adds is a model in which the rule can be watched working and, more usefully, watched failing. A heuristic you can break on purpose is worth more than a law you have to take on trust.

Self-studyWhat "hours removed" means, and what it does not4 min▶

Hours removed is a rate, in hours a week: the people on a task, times the hours a week each spends on it, times the share of that work a machine can take over. For Serai's content studio, 40 people x 30 hours x 0.35 automatable = 420 hours a week. Multiply by the loaded hour and subtract what the tool costs to run and you have the value of the use case in dollars a week. Every figure in that chain is an assumption you can argue about, which is the point of writing it down.

What it does not mean is headcount cut. An enabler that removes 420 hours from the studio and dismisses 14 people has turned a scaling advantage into a one-off saving and a morale problem. The margin case is that the same 40 people now carry more brands: the next twelve brands need almost no new content hours, so retainers grow and the drain does not. Hours removed is capacity for more brands at the same cost, and it compounds; a redundancy does not.

The honest caveat: the automatable share is where every sizing lies to its author. Thirty-five percent of a content studio's week is a defensible number for repetitive listing copy, resizing and cutdowns; it is not a defensible number for creative direction or brand negotiation. In the build-along you will estimate that share for your own teams, and the instruction is to be mean with it.

Real world

Even the two dollars is on a number somebody defined. Sea Limited's annual report defines Shopee's GMV as the value of orders on the marketplace, says the calculation "includes shipping and other charges", and counts an order "regardless of whether the transaction is settled or if the item is returned". An enabler paid 2 percent of a GMV like that is paid on shipping fees and on returned goods until someone writes down otherwise. The hour route has no such ambiguity: an hour not spent is an hour not spent. That certainty is part of the case for it, and session 6 is where the GMV definition gets written down once.

Part 2 · covers sizing ten use cases from one assumptions block and the four roadmap orders

Ten use cases, sized from one block

6 min live

Ten use cases sit on Serai's constructed roadmap. Each is sized the same way, from one block of assumptions: people, hours, automatable share, build weeks, run cost, and where a payer exists, an attach fee or a GMV uplift. The bench in analyst session 8 runs the block; the numbers below are what it prints at the defaults. Every one of them is constructed, and they are here to show a shape, not to describe any real enabler.

LiveWhy pricing optimisation is big in GMV and small for Serai2 min▶

Pricing optimisation is the use case with the largest number on the page and the smallest bar. At the defaults it lifts brand GMV by 2 percent, which on $45 million a quarter is $69,230.77 a week of extra sales for the brands. Serai's 2 percent share of that is $1,384.62. Add the six hours a week it removes from three pricing analysts ($108 at $18 an hour), subtract $500 a week to run the model, and Serai nets $992.62 a week. Ninety-eight cents in every dollar of the uplift is the brand's.

None of that makes pricing a bad product. It makes it a product for the brand, and a brand should pay for it. The moment there is a payer, an attach fee for pricing moves its bar up like any other line in the block. Without one, an enabler that builds pricing first with its own money has spent fourteen build weeks lifting somebody else's GMV. That is the whole of the anti-lever, and it is why the four roadmap orders below end the way they do.

LiveThe four orders, and the ranking the bench refuses to make3 min▶

The bench runs the same ten use cases in four orders, one build squad at $9,000 a week, eight quarters, and prints the net to Serai. The single customer view is blocked in every order, because the identity gate in session 4 does not clear a customer grain; the bench builds it and it returns nothing.

Read the two ends first. Pricing first adds the most GMV of any order, $7,310,769, and returns $511,972 less to Serai than hours first, paying back two quarters later. It pays the brand. Single customer view first spends sixteen build weeks on a use case the gate blocks and ends lowest of the four.

Now read the two at the top, carefully. Hours first and brand intelligence first land within 3.2 percent of each other, $43,451 apart. That gap is made entirely of attach revenue the assumptions block stipulates: how much a brand would pay for a SKU view or a creator scorecard is a number somebody typed, not a number anybody measured. So the bench does not rank hours first above brand intelligence first, and neither should you on its evidence. The argument for starting with hours is a different kind of argument: certainty. Removing hours needs nobody's permission and no buyer. Selling intelligence needs both, and session 5 is about earning them.

Self-studyThe assumptions block, and why one block beats ten spreadsheets4 min▶

The assumptions block is the small set of inputs every number on this page is computed from. Company-wide: the loaded hour ($18), GMV a quarter ($45,000,000), Serai's GMV share (2 percent), the build squad's weekly cost ($9,000), thirteen-week quarters, an eight-quarter horizon, and the identity gate's threshold (0.90). Per use case: people, hours a week, automatable share, build weeks, run cost a week, attach fee a quarter where a payer exists, and GMV uplift where the use case lifts sales. Ten rows, one block, and the value of every use case falls out of the same formula:

hours removed x loaded hour + attach a week + GMV a week x uplift x Serai's share - run cost

The usual alternative is ten business cases in ten spreadsheets, each built by the team that wants the use case, each with its own loaded hour, its own quarter length and its own idea of how much brands will pay. They cannot be compared, which is convenient for whoever is loudest. One block makes every case share every assumption, so when the studio lead says the automatable share of content is 50 percent and not 35, the argument is about one cell and everybody can see what it does to every bar. The block is a governance device dressed as a calculator.

Two rules for keeping it honest. First, a stipulated number stays labelled as one: the attach fees in Serai's block are guesses until a brand signs, and the bench's refusal to rank on them is the model for how to treat every such cell. Second, the block is edited in the open, with a date and a reason, so that when a bar moves everybody knows why. The analyst track turns that second rule into code in session 7.

Constructed case

Serai's first roadmap argument, constructed. Serai's commercial director wants pricing first: it is the use case brands ask about, and the GMV number is enormous. The head of the content studio wants the content engine and cannot get a hearing, because "we would save some hours" does not sound like $7 million. The block ends the meeting in ten minutes. Pricing first: $860,315 to Serai after eight quarters. Content engine first, inside hours first: $1,372,287. The director's point survives in a different form: pricing is a product, so find the brands who will pay an attach fee for it, and its bar rises. Nobody had to be wrong; they had to be reading the same sheet.

Part 3 · covers the setting where the rule flips, and how to know which side you are on

The crossover: when the rule stops holding

4 min live

A rule that cannot be broken is not a rule, it is a preference. The bench lets you break this one. Raise Serai's GMV share until the business is paid mostly on sales rather than on effort, and the same ten use cases reorder themselves. The anti-lever is an anti-lever for a fee-based enabler; for a business paid on GMV share it is the lever. Knowing which side of that line you stand on is the only thing tonight asks you to be sure of.

LiveRead your own fee split before you read anyone's roadmap3 min▶

Open the assumptions block in the bench and change one input: Serai's GMV share. At the default 0.02, hours first returns $1,372,287 and pricing first $860,315. Set the share to 0.06 and the pricing uplift to 0.06, and pricing first prints $1,900,438 against $1,612,656 for hours first. Set the share to 0.10, and pricing first wins from an uplift of only 0.04: $2,068,254 against $1,659,180. Nothing about the use cases changed. What changed is how the business is paid.

So the first question for your own roadmap is not "which use case" but "what is our split". Take last quarter's revenue and divide it into money that moved with brand GMV and money that did not. Session 1's homework asked your finance lead for exactly those two numbers. If most of your revenue arrives whatever the brands sell, you are a fee-based enabler and hours come first. If most of it arrives as a share of sales, you are closer to a brand, and the use cases that lift GMV are your lever, pricing among them. Hybrids sit in between, and the honest move is to run your own share through the block rather than to guess which side you are on.

One caution that carries over from the 3.2 percent finding: the crossover cells are as constructed as everything else, and an uplift of 6 percent from pricing is an assumption, not a promise. What the crossover establishes is the shape of the rule, not the exact share at which any real business should flip. That share is yours to compute from your own block.

Self-studyThree roadmaps that put pricing first, and what their revenue model said4 min▶

Three constructed, generic cases, no real company behind any of them. Each put pricing optimisation at the top of its roadmap. Judge each by its revenue model, not by the outcome.

  • The house-brand operator. A company that sells its own brands on the marketplaces and keeps the goods margin. Its revenue is a share of GMV in the purest sense: all of it. Pricing first was right for it, and the hours-removed argument was close to irrelevant, because its cost base was stock and marketing, not a rota. On the grid it lives at the bottom row, share near 1.0.
  • The fee-based enabler that copied the operator's deck. Retainers made up most of its revenue, its GMV share was small, and it built pricing first because the operator down the road had. It spent its first three quarters of build capacity lifting brand GMV it kept two percent of, while its content studio kept growing headcount with every new brand. Pricing first was wrong for it, for exactly the reason in Part 2: no payer. The fix was not to abandon pricing but to sell it, with an attach fee, to the brands who wanted it.
  • The performance-fee agency. Paid a large share of incremental GMV above a baseline, with a small fixed fee. Pricing first was right for it, and its roadmap looked like the operator's despite it owning no goods, because its fee split put it on the same row of the grid. This is the case that shows the rule is about the split, not about the business's label.

The same use case was right twice and wrong once, and the one thing that predicted it every time was the share of revenue that moved with GMV. Nothing else on the roadmap did.

Constructed case

The renewal that moved Serai across the grid, constructed. Suppose Serai's twelve largest brands renegotiate at renewal: lower retainers, a 6 percent GMV share, and a bonus above target. Serai's finance lead would say the company just got more aligned with its brands, and she would be right. Its head of data should say something else the same afternoon: at a 6 percent share the pricing row on the grid changes colour, and the roadmap that was correct in the morning is worth re-running before the board pack goes out. A fee split is a roadmap input, and it can change at a signature.

Build-along

Size your top five use cases

★ 20 min · everyone builds

You need session 1's money map and your rota, nothing else. Five steps, one sheet, and every number on it is one you can defend to the person who runs the team. If you do not run an enabler, do it for Serai with the figures on this page; the worked lines below are Serai's, and your numbers will differ.

List your hour sinks from session 1's money map. Block 4 of that map ranked your five biggest teams by people times hours a week. Copy them across as rows. If one team does two distinct jobs, one repetitive and one not, split the row now, because the automatable share in the next step will be very different for each.

★ Step 1 · rows from the money mapContent studio 40 people x 30 h = 1,200 h a week Customer service 24 people x 38 h = 912 h a week Store operations 16 people x 30 h = 480 h a week Ad operations 12 people x 30 h = 360 h a week Live team 8 people x 25 h = 200 h a week

Estimate the automatable share, and be mean with it. For each row, what fraction of the week is repetition a machine can draft, resize, look up or pre-check? Twenty-five to forty percent is the honest range for most operational teams; anything above forty needs the team lead's signature next to it. Serai's block uses 0.35 for content, 0.30 for service and ads, 0.25 for store ops. Write the number down with the reason.

★ Step 2 · the share, with its reasonContent 0.35 listing copy in 5 languages, resizing, cutdowns; not creative direction Service 0.30 "where is my parcel" and returns lookups; not complaints Store ops 0.25 campaign set-up and price-list uploads; not negotiations Ad ops 0.30 pre-flight checks and pacing alerts; not creative Live 0.35 post-stream scoring and clip tagging; not hosting

Compute hours removed x loaded cost, minus run cost. Hours removed is people x hours x share. Multiply by your loaded hour (Serai's is $18, constructed; yours comes from payroll divided by paid hours, including overheads), then subtract what the tool costs to run each week. This is the number that stays with you whatever the brands sell.

★ Step 3 · Serai's content line, worked40 people x 30 h x 0.35 = 420 hours removed a week 420 h x $18 loaded = $7,560 minus run cost = $600 value a week to Serai = $6,960

Add attach and GMV-share lines only where a payer exists. If brands would pay for the output, add the fee spread over the quarter's thirteen weeks. If the use case lifts GMV, add your share of the lift, not the lift itself. If there is no signed or clearly willing payer, the line is zero and stays zero until there is. Serai's live stream scoring carries a $40,000 a quarter attach because brands running streams would pay for the scorecard; its content engine carries none.

★ Step 4 · the live line, with an attach8 people x 25 h x 0.35 = 70 hours x $18 = $1,260 minus run cost = $300 attach $40,000 a quarter / 13 weeks = $3,076.92 value a week to Serai = $4,036.92 Pricing, for contrast: 3 x 10 x 0.20 = 6 h x $18 = $108 brand GMV lift $45M / 13 x 0.02 = $69,230.77 a week, Serai's 2% = $1,384.62 minus run $500 = $992.62 to Serai

Order by value a week, then mark the rows that need a grain the marketplaces mask. Sort column 5 largest first. Then, for each row, write down the finest grain it needs to work: order, store, SKU or customer. Store and SKU are Serai's own keys and hold. Customer grain needs the buyer's identity, which all three marketplaces mask on orders they fulfil; session 4 is about exactly how much survives. Any row that needs it gets a flag, and the flag beats the value: the single customer view is worth $4,362.46 a week on paper and nothing in practice.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · At Serai's default assumptions, why does pricing optimisation pay the brand rather than Serai?

The lift is real and large, and it is the brand's money by construction of the fee split. Serai's share is two cents in the dollar, so after the six hours it removes and the $500 run cost, Serai nets $992.62 a week. Build weeks matter to payback, not to who is paid; pricing needs SKU grain, which holds; and it does remove a few hours, just not many.

2 · What single change to the assumptions block makes pricing first overtake hours first on the bench?

The rule is about how the business is paid. Raise the share of revenue that moves with brand GMV and the use cases that lift GMV become Serai's lever; at a 0.10 share pricing wins from a 0.04 uplift. A dearer hour helps hours first, not pricing; squad cost and horizon move every order together.

3 · Hours first returns $1,372,287 and brand intelligence first $1,415,738. Why does the bench refuse to rank one above the other?

A ranking is only as good as the input that decides it, and $43,451 of stipulated attach fees decides this one. The bench says so instead of pretending. The case for hours first is certainty, not the bench's number: it needs no permission and no buyer. Brand intelligence clears the gate (SKU and store grain hold), the two orders add different GMV, and payback quarter is not a ranking rule.
